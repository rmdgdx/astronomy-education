Foreword

From the principal investigator

Every great discovery begins with a good question — and a map to guide the ones that follow.

For a long time, astronomy in the Philippines has advanced on the strength of passion more than of any shared map. Our observatories, our classrooms, and our night-sky gatherings have all been carried forward by people who love the discipline, yet the field has never had a dedicated agenda to point that love in a common direction. This companion, drawn from the doctoral research on the Astronomy Education Research Agenda, is offered as a small step toward such a map.

An agenda is, at its heart, a compass for scholarly exploration. For Philippine astronomy, this one is meant to do several things at once: to give our astronomy and science-and-technology community a shared and deliberate focus; to help build a well-established pool of experts and skills grounded in research that practitioners can genuinely use; and to open astronomy to the many Filipino learners — including the underrepresented and the underreached — for whom the night sky should always be a door, never a wall. If it helps even a few teachers find their next project, a few students dream a little further, and a few institutions invest in the telescopes and training our young scientists deserve, it will have served its purpose.

I hope, too, that it proves useful beyond the classroom — that curriculum designers find in it a basis for innovative teaching, that scientists and professional astronomers come to see astronomy education as work worthy of their careers, that pre-service teachers take up projects with organizations and agencies across the country, and that policymakers and administrators recognize the value of steady support. In particular, I hope it serves the teachers and faculty who are teaching or doing research in Earth and Space Science, giving them a ready well of ideas, questions, and projects to draw from. Astronomy has always been a collective act of looking up; a research agenda simply invites us to look up together, and in the same direction.

None of this would exist without the generosity of many, and it is my deepest privilege to thank them. My profound gratitude goes to the scientists and professional astronomers — the astrophysicists, space scientists, and astrobiologists — whose expertise shaped every priority; to the researchers and research-agenda experts who lent their wisdom to the model behind this work; and to the astronomy faculty and science teachers who carry this discipline into real classrooms every day. I am equally indebted to the astronomy organizations, institutions, and government agencies — DOST–PAGASA foremost among them — whose long stewardship of astronomy in the Philippines made this agenda possible.

To the amateur astronomers who keep wonder alive in our communities, to the school administrators who make room for this work, and above all to the students for whom the whole endeavor exists — thank you. Every focus-group voice, every shared idea, and every quiet act of encouragement is woven into these pages. May this small book honor that trust, and may it help the light of astronomy education in the Philippines shine a little brighter, and reach a little farther, for many years to come.

Dr. Ryan Manuel D. GuidoPrincipal investigator, with Dr. Antriman V. Orleans, co-investigator

Standing on the shoulders of a shared vision

Before the agenda — the mandates that guide it

No research agenda is written in isolation. This one takes its bearings from a constellation of mandates — global goals, an astronomy roadmap, a national plan, and a science agenda. Aligning with them ensures the work is purposeful, relevant, and ready to make a real difference for Filipino learners and communities.

UNESCO Sustainable Development Goals (SDG)

The UNESCO Sustainable Development Goals are the world's shared blueprint for a fairer, greener, and more prosperous future — seventeen goals adopted by member states to be reached by 2030. Because the SDGs form the very foundation of the Philippine Development Plan and of the country's sectoral plans, every well-crafted research agenda begins here. For astronomy education, SDG 4 — Quality Education — is the guiding star, calling for inclusive, equitable, and lifelong learning for all.

IAU Strategic Plan 2020–2030

The International Astronomical Union's Strategic Plan sets a clear, decade-long course for using astronomy as a force for good. It focuses its energy on six SDGs — Quality Education (4), Gender Equality and Women Empowerment (5), Decent Work and Economic Growth (8), Reduced Inequalities (10), Sustainable Cities and Communities (11), and Partnership for the Goals (17). Through its Office of Astronomy for Education, the IAU champions teacher training, capacity building, and inclusive outreach that this agenda proudly echoes.

Philippine Development Plan (PDP) 2023–2028

Rooted in AmBisyon Natin 2040 — the collective vision and aspirations of the Filipino people — the Philippine Development Plan charts a medium-term path toward inclusive growth, a high-trust society, and a globally competitive knowledge economy. An astronomy education research agenda that speaks the language of the PDP helps ensure its findings are useful, relevant, and genuinely felt by communities across the country.

DOST Harmonized National R&D Agenda (HNRDA)

The Department of Science and Technology's Harmonized National Research and Development Agenda shapes research priorities across every discipline. In astronomy, it connects celestial study with real-world challenges — linking space weather and solar activity to climate resilience, disaster preparedness, and the protection of critical infrastructure. It reminds us that looking up and caring for those on the ground are one and the same pursuit.

Working hand in hand

This agenda also grows in the good company of national and regional partners: the Commission on Higher Education (CHED) with its relevant and responsive research thrusts; the DOST Science Education Institute (DOST-SEI), nurturing the next generation of scientists; and the ASEAN Higher Education Space 2025, which links our aspirations with those of our neighbours. Together, these mandates form the constellation by which the agenda finds its bearings.

Why this agenda exists

Rationale of the research agenda

Astronomy is a young and fast-growing science in the Philippines. It found a place in our colleges through a government memorandum — CHED Memorandum Order No. 75, series of 2017 — but that memo never spelled out exactly what should be taught or researched. That gap is the reason this agenda exists.

This is the first astronomy education research agenda made for the country. It gathers the themes and questions worth studying so that teachers and researchers have a clear place to begin, and it offers a simple model for building a research agenda in the first place. Its aim is practical and hopeful: to make astronomy teaching, learning, and research more engaging — drawing on planetariums, observatories, and laboratories — and to do so in a way that experts, researchers, and faculty have reviewed and stand behind.

What this agenda could change

Implications of the research agenda

Used well, this agenda could lift science education across the Philippines, in both schools and universities. It puts students first and welcomes learners of every background, so that astronomy becomes fair and open to all, and it encourages modern, digital ways of learning that prepare young people for today’s world.

Its reach goes further still. When teachers are trained in astronomy, lessons improve; when assessments are shared and standardized, results become easier to trust. The agenda points to the real economic value of astronomy, and to partnerships among schools, research institutions, and government that can make Filipino work globally competitive. And by caring about citizen science, the environment, and indigenous knowledge of the sky, it serves not only the classroom but the wider community and culture. In short, it can help make science education here more inclusive, more innovative, and more respected around the world.

What this agenda is — and isn’t

Limitations of the research agenda

This agenda is a guide, not a rulebook. It points a direction rather than dictating exactly what anyone must do, and it was built at a time when research on astronomy education in the Philippines is still young and scarce — so there was not a great deal of earlier work to draw upon.

A few honest boundaries are worth naming. The country has only a small number of professional astronomers, which limited how much expert knowledge could feed into the work, and because few people here have direct experience teaching astronomy, the group who shaped the agenda was small. By design, it also leaves out pure astronomical research pursued for its own sake, focusing instead on the parts of astronomy that can be taught and studied in basic and higher education. Naming these limits is not a weakness but an invitation: this is a living starting point that future work can test, grow, and improve.

A.1

What are the experiences of students in learning astronomy?

1Student academic achievement in astronomy

This topic examines how well students perform in astronomy and what shapes those results. Researchers might gather grades, test scores, or competition outcomes and relate them to factors such as prior schooling, resources, and teaching style. The findings reveal where learners thrive and where support is most needed.

Possible variables
Independent — prior schooling, learning resources, teaching approach, study habits; Dependent — astronomy achievement; Controls — grade level, socioeconomic status.
Suggested methodology
Descriptive-correlational. A descriptive-correlational design fits, since the aim is to describe astronomy achievement and the learner and school factors around it and examine how they relate rather than to manipulate them. Validated scales and outcome measures are administered to a representative sample without any intervention. The pattern of associations shows which factors most strongly track with outcomes and sets up later experimental work.
Statistics and data analytics
Means and standard deviations; Pearson r (or Spearman rho); multiple regression to rank predictors.
2Investigating learning environments and opportunities which influence students’ prior knowledge, misconceptions, and general understanding

This research looks closely at the classrooms, homes, and communities that shape what students already believe about the sky. Classroom observations, concept inventories, and short interviews can surface both accurate ideas and stubborn misconceptions. The result is a clearer map of the starting points teachers must build upon.

Possible variables
Key variables — features of the learning environment (home, classroom, community); learners’ prior knowledge and misconceptions; general understanding.
Suggested methodology
Explanatory sequential mixed-methods. An explanatory sequential mixed-methods design suits this topic: a concept inventory first measures prior knowledge and misconceptions across a sample, then interviews explain how particular environments shaped them. The quantitative phase locates where misconceptions cluster; the qualitative phase uncovers why. Together they give both breadth and depth about learners’ starting points.
Statistics and data analytics
Quantitative — frequencies, mean/SD, misconception-frequency indices; Qualitative — thematic analysis; a joint display to integrate results.
3Investigating student barriers to learning astronomy

This study identifies the conceptual, emotional, and practical obstacles that make astronomy feel out of reach. Surveys and focus-group discussions with learners can bring these barriers into the open. Understanding them is the first step toward lessons that gently remove them.

Possible variables
Key variables — conceptual, affective, and practical barriers; learner background; access to resources.
Suggested methodology
Descriptive survey with qualitative support. A descriptive survey design, supported by focus-group discussions, is appropriate for mapping the barriers learners face. A validated questionnaire captures how common each barrier is, while focus groups reveal their texture and interconnections. Stratified sampling ensures learners of different backgrounds are fairly represented.
Statistics and data analytics
Frequencies, percentages, weighted means, and ranking of barriers; chi-square for group differences; thematic analysis of focus-group data.
4Astronomy as a program to develop students’ literacy and critical thinking skills

This topic explores how astronomy lessons can strengthen reading, reasoning, and evidence-based thinking. A comparison of astronomy-rich instruction with usual practice can measure gains in literacy and critical thinking. The study would show whether the wonders of the cosmos also sharpen everyday thinking.

Possible variables
Independent — astronomy-integrated instruction; Dependent — scientific literacy and critical-thinking scores; Controls — prior ability, reading level.
Suggested methodology
Quasi-experimental (pretest–posttest control group). A quasi-experimental pretest–posttest control-group design suits this topic. Two comparable intact classes are used — one taught through an astronomy-integrated literacy programme and the other through conventional instruction — and both take equivalent pre- and post-tests so that changes in literacy and critical thinking can be traced to the approach. Matching the groups on prior ability keeps the comparison fair, making the design practical for schools while still supporting cause-and-effect claims.
Statistics and data analytics
Paired and independent-samples t-tests; ANCOVA controlling pretest scores; Cohen’s d for effect size; normality and homogeneity checks first.

Salient features of the research agenda

What the agenda values most

As the agenda took shape, four clear features rose to the top — each one tied closely to the Core Research Goals. Together they capture, at a glance, what this agenda values most as it works to advance astronomy education in the Philippines.

  1. A student-centered, many-sided approach

    Learning that begins with the student. It welcomes diverse and inclusive astronomy education and embraces Digital 5.0 ways of learning, so lessons fit each learner’s needs and spark real curiosity.

  2. Teacher training and professional development

    Well-prepared, well-equipped teachers. This means real astronomy tools — telescopes, instruments, and equipment — and assessment tools standardized for the Philippine setting, so teaching keeps getting better.

  3. Scientific employment and economic growth

    Astronomy as a source of jobs and opportunity. It encourages collaboration among industries, research institutions, and agencies, and aims for curricula and research that can compete on the world stage.

  4. Citizen science and environmental conservation

    Astronomy that serves society. It makes room for everyday people to take part, and it honors cultural and indigenous astronomy while supporting the laws, regulations, and policies that protect the field and the night sky.
